A wasp nest left alone through summer doesn't stay small. According to the British Pest Control Association, a nest can hold up to 5,000 wasps at its peak, growing from something the size of a golf ball in spring to a nest as large as a football, an armchair, or in a bad year, even something closer to a washing machine, by late summer. A problem that starts as a handful of wasps around the shed in April can be a serious hazard by August, which is exactly why the busiest month for call outs is also the one where DIY attempts go wrong most often.

This page covers what we actually treat, how to tell a wasp nest apart from bees or hornets (more common a mix up than people expect), what a professional visit involves, and what it costs. Why Wasps Are a Problem

Wasps only sting defensively, to protect the nest or the queen, not at random, which is worth knowing before panic sets in. The real danger comes when a nest is disturbed: wasps release an alarm pheromone when threatened, which signals others nearby to join in, so a single sting near an active nest can quickly become several. For most people that's painful but not dangerous. For anyone with an allergy, it's a different story, insect stings are responsible for a significant proportion of adult anaphylaxis cases in the UK each year, and multiple stings from a disturbed nest raise that risk sharply even without a known allergy.

Most UK wasp stings come from two species, the Common Wasp and the German Wasp, both of which build nests in the same kinds of spots around Derby homes: lofts, wall cavities, sheds, garages, and occasionally underground in old rockeries or compost heaps.

What Damage Can a Wasp Nest Cause?

Wasp nests are built from chewed wood fibre, a papery, honeycomb-textured material, so they rarely cause structural damage to a property the way rodents or wood-boring insects can. The risk is almost entirely to people rather than the building itself, an established nest near a doorway, a garden used daily, or a loft space that gets visited for storage is a genuine hazard, not just an eyesore.

Size is the clearest sign of how established a nest has become. A nest just started in spring holds only the founding queen and is roughly golf-ball sized. By mid-summer, as workers take over building and foraging, it can reach the size of a football. By August or September, a mature nest at peak population can be as large as an armchair, sometimes bigger, holding thousands of wasps rather than dozens.

Is It Definitely a Wasp Nest? Bees and Hornets Explained

Getting the identification right matters, because wasps, bees and hornets aren't all treated the same way, and one of them generally shouldn't be treated at all.

Bees. Honey bees in particular are commonly mistaken for wasps by the public, they're rounder, hairier, and move more slowly. Reputable pest controllers, following BPCA guidance, don't treat bees except where there's a genuine risk to health, since bee populations are in serious decline and they're vital pollinators. If you've got a honey bee swarm, the usual advice is to contact the British Beekeepers Association for a local swarm collector, who will often collect it free of charge, rather than have it exterminated. Bumblebee and solitary bee colonies are normally best left alone entirely, they decline naturally by the end of July and won't return to the same spot the following year.

European hornets. Britain's native hornet is larger than a wasp but generally less aggressive, with brown-tipped legs, long yellow antennae, and a mostly yellow abdomen with an obvious waist. They're treated in much the same way as a wasp nest.

Asian hornets. This is the one worth knowing about specifically. The Asian hornet (Vespa velutina) is a non-native invasive species, smaller than our native hornet, with an orange face, yellow-tipped legs, and a dark, velvety body. It isn't yet established in Derbyshire, sightings so far have concentrated in southern England, but numbers have been rising and awareness matters. If you genuinely suspect an Asian hornet, the correct step is to report it, ideally with a photo, to the GB Non-Native Species Secretariat, not simply have it treated privately. It's a national monitoring requirement, since Asian hornets are a serious threat to honeybee populations. If you're not sure what you're looking at, that's completely normal, call us and describe it and we'll help you work out what it actually is before recommending anything.

Signs You Have a Wasp Nest

  • A steady stream of wasps flying to and from one spot, especially in the morning and evening
  • A papery, honeycomb-textured nest visible in a loft, shed, or under the eaves
  • A low buzzing or rustling sound coming from a wall cavity or roof space
  • Wasps appearing indoors repeatedly from the same direction or gap
  • A grey, paper-like structure roughly the size of a football or larger, usually first spotted from the outside of a building

How Do You Get Rid of a Wasp Nest?

DIY wasp nest removal is one of the more dangerous jobs a homeowner can attempt. Shop-bought sprays and foams can provoke a defensive response from the whole colony rather than dealing with it cleanly, and reaching a nest tucked into a loft void or under eaves often means working at height, close to an active colony, without the protective clothing or professional-strength product a trained technician would use.

The only genuinely low-risk time to deal with a small nest without professional help is very early in the season, while it holds just the founding queen, or after the colony has naturally died off in autumn and the nest has been abandoned. Anywhere in between, when a nest is active and growing through the summer months, is worth leaving to a professional visit rather than risking multiple stings for a job that usually costs less than people expect.

A phone call first is usually enough to work out what's needed. Describe where the nest is and roughly how big it looks, and we can normally confirm a fixed price and next available slot before anyone comes out, with no separate site-visit charge just to quote.

How We Effectively Remove Wasp Nests

Most nests are treated and dealt with in a single call out, usually within the hour of a technician arriving. The nest is treated on site with a professional-strength insecticide applied directly to the entrance, and wasps clear over the following day or two as returning workers make contact with it and carry it back into the nest.

We won't remove the physical nest structure on the day itself, disturbing it too early can scatter a still-active colony rather than clear it cleanly, so it's left in place to be removed once activity has genuinely stopped, if you'd like that done as a follow-up. Treatment follows BPCA guidelines throughout, and if activity hasn't cleared within a few days of the visit, we'll come back and re-treat it at no extra charge, rather than leaving you to chase up a job that wasn't finished properly the first time.

Loft, shed, eaves, or underground, if it's on your property in Derby or the surrounding areas, we can usually get to it same day between April and September, when nests are at their most active and most dangerous to leave alone.

Common questions

How long until the wasps are gone after treatment?

Most nests clear within 24 to 48 hours as returning wasps come into contact with the treatment. It can look more active for the first few hours before it drops off.

Is it safe to stay in the house during treatment?

Yes, though we'll usually suggest keeping windows and doors near the nest shut for the rest of the day while it takes effect.

What if it's still active after treatment?

It's uncommon, but if activity hasn't cleared within a few days, let us know and we'll come back to re-treat it, no separate call out charge for a genuine return visit on the same nest.

What if I find a second nest nearby?

It's not unusual to find one once the first is treated, wasps often nest in similar spots on the same property. Let us know and we'll deal with that one too.

How do I know if it's actually bees, not wasps?

Bees are rounder, hairier and move more slowly than wasps, and honey bees in particular are often mistaken for wasps by the public. If there's any doubt, describe what you're seeing, including colour and how they're behaving, and we'll help identify it before recommending anything.

What if I think it's an Asian hornet?

Asian hornets are a non-native invasive species, smaller and darker than our native hornets, and are not yet established in Derbyshire, though sightings have increased across southern England. If you genuinely suspect one, report it with a photo to the GB Non-Native Species Secretariat rather than treating it privately, this is a national monitoring requirement, not just advice. If you're not sure what you're looking at, call us and we'll help you tell the difference.
