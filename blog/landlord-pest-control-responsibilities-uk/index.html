Landlord Pest Control Responsibilities in the UK

Pest control responsibility between landlords and tenants isn't covered by one single law, it's built from several overlapping pieces of legislation. Here's what actually applies in England, and how responsibility typically splits between landlord and tenant.

The Legal Framework

The Housing Act 2004 established the Housing Health and Safety Rating System (HHSRS), a framework local authorities use to assess housing conditions. Pests and vermin are one of the prescribed hazards under this system, and serious cases are classed as Category 1 hazards, which a local authority must act on.

The Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) Act 2018 goes further, requiring rented homes to be fit for human habitation and free from serious hazards, including vermin, both when a tenancy starts and throughout it. This applies to most tenancies with a fixed term of less than seven years.

Section 11 of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 requires landlords to keep a property's structure and exterior in good repair, which matters directly for pest control since damaged vents, gaps and deteriorating brickwork are exactly how rodents get in. A severe, ongoing infestation can also count as a statutory nuisance under the Environmental Protection Act 1990.

When It's the Landlord's Responsibility

Broadly, if an infestation is linked to the condition or design of the property, poor repair, damaged airbricks, gaps around pipework, inadequate waste storage, it falls to the landlord to resolve. A landlord generally isn't liable if the infestation is wholly or mainly caused by the tenant's own actions, most often rubbish left to build up, or if the tenant refuses reasonable access for treatment.

When It's the Tenant's Responsibility

Tenants are typically responsible where their own behaviour has attracted or caused the problem, commonly poor waste disposal, leaving food out, or a breach of the tenancy agreement's cleanliness terms. Some landlords also draw a practical distinction in their own policies between pest types, treating a rat infestation (often linked to structural entry points) as their responsibility while treating a more isolated mouse or insect issue differently, though this varies by landlord and tenancy agreement rather than being set in statute.

What This Means in Practice

For landlords, the safest approach is a documented proofing survey between tenancies and a quick, professional response to any tenant report, since delay is what tends to turn a minor issue into a Category 1 hazard and a local authority enforcement notice. For tenants, reporting a problem promptly and in writing protects you either way, whoever ends up responsible for the fix.

Common questions

Does this apply to all rented properties?

The Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) Act 2018 applies to most tenancies under seven years. Older or different tenancy types may fall under slightly different rules, so check your specific agreement if you're unsure.

What should a landlord do between tenancies?

A proofing survey before a new tenant moves in, checking for the entry points and disrepair issues most likely to cause problems later, is the most effective preventative step. See our commercial pest control page for details.
