Why Rats Are a Problem

Rats aren't just unpleasant to have around, they're a genuine health risk. They can carry leptospirosis (Weil's disease), spread through contact with urine-contaminated water or surfaces, along with salmonella from contaminated food and surfaces. Gnawing on electrical wiring is also a recognised house fire risk, not just a cosmetic annoyance.

They breed quickly too. A problem that looks small when first noticed, a few droppings, a scratching sound at night, is often bigger by the time it's treated, which is normal and not a sign anything was missed earlier.

Why Rats Are Common in Derby Specifically

Derby's rivers, canals and green spaces aren't just scenery, they're exactly how rats move through a city largely unseen. The Derwent corridor runs straight through the centre, and Derby Arboretum, one of the first planned public parks in the country when it was gifted to the town in 1840, sits alongside Allestree Park, Darley Park, Chaddesden Park, Alvaston Park, Normanton Park and Osmaston Park, all of which act as safe harbourage for rats to nest before foraging outward into nearby gardens, sheds, basements and bins.

Once they've found cover along a green corridor or riverbank, rats forage for whatever's nearby: food scraps, spilt bins, pet food left outside, bird seed on a garden feeder. Derby's dense hospitality scene around Cathedral Quarter and Friar Gate, with its pubs, restaurants and takeaways, adds another layer of opportunity, since scavenging around food waste is exactly what draws rodents into busier parts of the city, not just quiet residential streets.

Housing age plays its part too. Older streets near the centre, much of it Victorian or interwar, give rats easy entry through original airbricks and ageing brickwork, while rat activity indoors typically peaks from autumn into winter as they move inside for warmth, though in a city as built-up as Derby, populations tend to stay active year-round rather than disappearing over a mild winter.

What Damage Can Rats Do?

Rats gnaw constantly to keep their front teeth from overgrowing, and that habit does real damage. Chewed electrical wiring is one of the most serious risks, since it's a recognised cause of house fires, not just a repair bill. They'll also gnaw through timber, pipework, plastic and insulation, and their gnawing on water pipes can lead to leaks that go unnoticed until there's damp or structural damage behind a wall.

For commercial properties, rat activity brings a different kind of cost. Contaminated stock, damaged packaging and the reputational risk of a rat sighting during trading hours can be far more expensive than the treatment itself.

Signs You Might Have Rats

  • Droppings near skirting boards, in cupboards, or in the loft, typically dark and around the size of a grain of rice or larger
  • Gnaw marks on wood, cables, or food packaging
  • Scratching noises in walls or ceilings, especially at night
  • A stale, musty smell in enclosed spaces
  • Greasy rub marks along walls where rats travel repeatedly
  • Burrows outside, often near sheds, compost heaps, riverbanks, or decking

How Do You Get Rid of Rats?

Shop bought rodenticide can reduce a rat problem, but it rarely clears one completely. Rats are cautious animals and often avoid bait that isn't placed correctly, and DIY bait left out in the open is a real risk to pets, wildlife and children if it isn't secured properly.

A proper approach starts with working out how they're getting in, not just putting bait down where they've been seen. Gaps around pipework, damaged airbricks and worn brickwork are the most common entry points in Derby's older housing stock, and until those are found and addressed, new rats can move in as fast as the old ones are cleared.

Reducing what's drawing them in helps too. Securing bins properly, clearing spilt bird seed rather than leaving it on the ground, storing pet food indoors, and not leaving food waste accessible overnight all reduce how attractive a property is in the first place, though this works alongside professional treatment rather than replacing it once rats are already established.

How We Effectively Remove Rats

Every visit starts with a full internal and external property check, not just a scattering of bait. We look at where rats are getting in, where they're nesting, and what's drawing them in before deciding on bait, traps, or both.

Bait is placed in tamper-resistant, child and pet safe stations, and we'll always talk you through exactly what's being used and where. A follow-up visit 10 to 14 days later is included as standard, to check bait uptake and confirm the property is clear, along with practical proofing advice so the same gap doesn't let the next lot in.

Common questions

How long does rat treatment take to work?

Most households see activity drop off within 5 to 7 days of the first visit, though it depends on the size of the infestation. The follow-up visit 10 to 14 days later confirms it's fully cleared.

Is the bait you use safe around pets and children?

Yes. All bait is placed in tamper-resistant stations that pets and children can't access, and we'll always talk you through exactly what's used and where before we start.

Can you stop rats getting in again?

We'll point out the likely entry points we find during the visit, such as gaps around pipework, airbricks, or damaged brickwork, so you know what to get sealed. That's included as standard, not sold as an extra.

Why does Derby seem to have so many rats near the river and parks?

Rivers, canals and green spaces act as natural corridors for rats to move through a city unseen, before foraging into nearby gardens, sheds and bins for food. It's a genuine pattern in Derby specifically, not just a general claim, since the Derwent corridor and the city's parks run through so much of it.

What if it's not fully cleared after the follow-up visit?

That's rare once proofing advice has been followed, but if activity is still ongoing we'll come back and reassess at no extra charge for that visit, rather than leaving you to book a separate job.
