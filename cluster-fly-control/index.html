Cluster flies gather in large numbers in lofts and upper rooms as the weather turns in autumn, looking for somewhere sheltered to spend the winter. Why Cluster Flies End Up in Your Loft, Not Your Kitchen

Cluster flies aren't drawn to food or rubbish like houseflies. Their larvae are parasites of earthworms, developing in soil around gardens and lawns during summer, which is an entirely different lifecycle to almost every other fly that ends up indoors. By late summer, the adult flies emerge with one goal: finding somewhere sheltered to spend the winter, and a loft or roof space is exactly that.

This explains a few things people often ask about. It's why cluster flies don't breed indoors or pose a food hygiene risk, since the actual breeding happens outdoors in soil months earlier. It's also why the same property can be targeted year after year while a neighbouring house is left alone, it usually comes down to which roof catches the most afternoon sun, south or west facing lofts warm up first as the weather turns and are exactly what a cluster fly is looking for.

What Damage Can Cluster Flies Cause?

Cluster flies don't cause structural damage, but large numbers gathering in one spot, often around a light fitting, window, or under insulating felt layers, can leave small dark spots on glass and walls where they've settled, and an unpleasant smell where any have died in place. They're also genuinely persistent once established, favouring sunken spotlights and loft voids as much as open roof space.

The bigger cost is usually the repeat visits people make trying to clear them themselves each autumn without treating the source, since vacuuming or spraying visible flies does nothing to stop the same loft attracting the next wave the following year.

Signs You Have Cluster Flies

  • Large numbers of sluggish flies appearing in a loft, upstairs room or window in autumn
  • Flies clustering in corners, light fittings, or sunken spotlights
  • At rest, the wingtips overlap over the body, a useful way to tell them apart from an ordinary housefly
  • A musty smell, or small dark spots on windows and walls where they've gathered
  • A sudden reappearance in spring as they try to leave through windows or light fixtures
  • Activity that dies down in winter and returns each autumn if untreated

How Do You Get Rid of Cluster Flies?

Because it's a seasonal pattern, timing matters more here than with most pests. Treating just before or as the first flies arrive in September or October is far more effective than waiting until a loft is already full of them in November. Outdoor perimeter treatments applied in late summer, before the flies move indoors, can also help reduce numbers reaching the building in the first place.

Vacuuming or spraying visible flies gives short term relief but doesn't stop the loft attracting more, since the flies are drawn to the space itself, its warmth and aspect, not anything that can be cleaned away. A treatment that reaches the roof void directly is needed to actually break the cycle, and professional treatment is usually timed to allow a cluster to establish fully before treating, since a handful of early arrivals is harder to fully clear than a settled group.

How We Effectively Remove Cluster Flies

We treat the loft or roof space directly where flies are gathering, timed for best effect just before the autumn cluster begins wherever possible, using a fogging treatment that reaches the whole void rather than just the flies visible near a window. We'll also advise on likely entry points given the property's aspect and construction, since south and west facing roofs are the most commonly affected.

Because the actual breeding happens outdoors in soil, not in your loft, there's no benefit to treating the garden itself, the earthworm population isn't something to control and doing so wouldn't reduce the number of adult flies seeking shelter regardless. If they weren't treated in autumn, the same cluster often reappears in spring, trying to get back out through windows and light fittings, and we can treat at that point too if needed. Common questions

Why does this keep happening every year in the same room?

Once a property's been used as an overwintering site, it tends to get reused. Treatment breaks that pattern, but a very exposed loft may need re-treating each autumn.

Are cluster flies harmful?

They don't bite, sting or spread disease, but large numbers indoors are unpleasant and can leave small dark spots on windows and walls where they've gathered.

What's the best time to book treatment?

Ideally early autumn, before the main cluster forms, though we still treat existing clusters through the winter.

Should I try to get rid of the earthworms in my garden?

No, and it wouldn't help even if you could. Earthworms are essential for healthy soil, and cluster fly larvae parasitising a small number of them in the surrounding area has no meaningful effect on your lawn. The flies you're dealing with indoors are adults that have already completed that stage months earlier, the problem now is the building, not the garden.

Why does my house get them and not my neighbour's?

It's usually about aspect rather than anything specific to your property's cleanliness. South or west facing roofs that catch the afternoon sun warm up first as the weather turns, which is exactly what draws cluster flies in to overwinter. A neighbouring house facing a different direction, or with a shadier roofline, may simply not present the same draw.
