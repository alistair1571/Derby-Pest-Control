The cat flea is by far the most common flea species found in UK homes, and despite the name, it's just as happy feeding on dogs, other pets, or people. A flea problem is almost never just the carpet where you've noticed them, and the aftercare matters just as much as the treatment itself, get it wrong and it can look like the job's failed when it actually hasn't.

Why Fleas Are a Problem

Fleas cause genuine discomfort, with bites that itch for days and can trigger allergic reactions in both people and pets. A single female can lay up to 50 eggs a day, which fall off wherever the host has been rather than staying in one nest, seeding carpets, sofas and pet bedding across an entire room within days. In a home with young children or anyone with sensitive skin, an untreated infestation can mean weeks of discomfort while numbers keep building.

Even properties without pets aren't immune. Fleas can arrive via a visiting animal, secondhand furniture, a previous tenant, or wildlife such as squirrels or birds nesting in the loft. Left untreated in pets, fleas can also transmit tapeworm, so it's worth mentioning to a vet if scratching has been going on for a while.

Why a New Home Can Have Fleas With No Pets at All

This genuinely surprises people, but it's one of the more common flea call outs we get. Flea pupae can lie dormant in carpets for months, sometimes well over a year, protected inside a cocoon that's naturally resistant to insecticide and vacuuming alike. They wait for a trigger: vibration, warmth and rising carbon dioxide, all signs of a potential host nearby. Moving into a property, even one that's been empty for a while, can be exactly that trigger, and pupae left behind by a previous occupant's pet can hatch in a rush within days of a new tenant or owner arriving.

It isn't a reflection of how clean a new home is. It's inherited, and it's treated the same way as any other flea problem, a whole property treatment rather than spot cleaning.

Signs You Have a Flea Problem

  • Itchy red bites, often around ankles or the waistline
  • Small jumping insects visible on carpets or pet bedding
  • Pets scratching or grooming noticeably more than usual
  • Tiny dark specks, known as flea dirt, on bedding or carpet, test it by placing specks on damp white paper, if it turns rusty red, that confirms it
  • Fleas spotted on socks after walking across a room

How Do You Get Rid of Fleas?

Treating a pet alone, without also treating the property, almost never solves a flea problem, since the vast majority of an infestation at any given time is eggs, larvae and pupae hidden in carpets and soft furnishings rather than adult fleas on the animal. Pupae in particular are naturally resistant to insecticide until the adult flea actually emerges from the cocoon, which is exactly why a single treatment can look like it's worked, then appear to fail a week or two later as the next wave hatches.

The aftercare matters as much as the treatment itself. Vacuum thoroughly before treatment, the vibration and disturbance helps stimulate dormant pupae into hatching so the insecticide can actually reach them, but then leave the treated carpets and floors alone. Vacuuming, sweeping or washing too soon removes the residual insecticide that's meant to keep killing newly emerging fleas over the following one to two weeks, and going in with the hoover after a couple of days is one of the most common reasons a professional treatment gets blamed for not working when it actually just got interrupted.

How We Effectively Remove Fleas

Treatment covers carpets, soft furnishings and skirting boards, not just the room where pets sleep, since flea eggs fall off wherever the pet has been and can seed a wider area than people expect. Common questions

Do you treat my pets as well?

No, we treat the property. Your vet or a pet-specific flea product handles the animal itself, and both need doing for it to fully clear.

How soon can I let pets back into treated rooms?

Usually once the treatment has dried, typically a couple of hours. We'll confirm on the day.

Why do I still see fleas a week or two after treatment?

Newly hatched fleas from surviving pupae are common for the first week or two, since the pupal stage is naturally resistant to insecticide until the flea actually emerges. This is expected, not a sign the treatment failed.

Should I vacuum after treatment?

No, and this catches a lot of people out. Vacuum thoroughly before we treat, it helps stimulate dormant pupae into hatching so the treatment can reach them, but avoid vacuuming, sweeping or washing the treated areas for around 10 to 14 days afterward. The insecticide needs to stay on the carpet and floor surfaces to keep killing newly emerging fleas during that window, and vacuuming it up early is one of the most common reasons a treatment appears to fail.

We've just moved into a new home and have fleas but no pets, why?

This is more common than people expect. Flea pupae can lie dormant in carpets for months, sometimes over a year, left behind by a previous occupant's pet, and hatch in a rush once they detect the vibration, warmth and carbon dioxide of new occupants moving in. It's not a reflection of your own home, it's inherited.

How do I know if it's actually flea dirt on the bedding?

A simple test: place the dark specks on a damp piece of white paper. If it turns a rusty red colour, that confirms flea dirt, since it's largely digested blood. Plain dirt or debris won't do that.
